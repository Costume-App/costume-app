# Costume Creations, VO script

Voice: Ava (en-US-AvaMultilingualNeural). Recorded against the Demo Theatre Co. org.

## Costume Creations (`intro`)

In the last video, we measured the cast of Twelfth Night. Now we will plan the costumes themselves, piece by piece. We will add the pieces each role needs, decide what to make and what to buy, hand the work off to makers, and work out how much fabric to buy and what the whole show will cost. By the end, every costume in the show will have a plan behind it.

## Add costume pieces (`add-pieces`)

Costumes are planned role by role, right alongside casting and measurements. Open a role and go to its "Costume" tab to start adding the pieces it needs.

> click: the Viola row, "Costume"

Type the name of each piece this role wears, like a doublet, breeches, and boots. More descriptive names help later, when the app estimates fabric for you. You can rename or remove any piece afterward, right from the same list.

> click "+ add piece"; type "Doublet" in "Piece name", "Add"; then "Breeches", "Boots"

## Photos and notes (`photos-notes`)

Each piece can carry up to six reference photos, like a sketch from the designer or a picture of the fabric. Attach one from your library, or shoot one on the spot from a phone.

> open "Doublet"; click the dashed add-photo tile; file chooser gets the generated sketch; hold on the thumbnail

Every piece also gets a notes box, for construction details or anything else the maker should know before they start sewing. Notes save the moment you click away, so there is nothing extra to remember or come back for.

> type in "Notes (optional)": "Green wool, brass buttons, fitted to the waist."

## Make it or buy it (`make-or-buy`)

For every performer, choose where each piece comes from. "Make" is the default choice, for anything your team will sew.

> Boots row for Maya Brooks: source select shown at "Make"

Choose "Purchase" instead for something you will buy rather than build, and enter what it costs so the show's total stays accurate. Two more choices skip building it fresh: "On hand" for something already in your closet, and "Shared" for reusing another performer's piece instead of duplicating it.

> Boots row for Maya Brooks: source "Purchase", type 45 into the purchase price field

## Assign makers (`makers`)

Pieces you are making can be handed to a maker. Each maker gets their own color, so you can see at a glance who is making what, across every role.

> Doublet: "Maker" select, choose "Priya Shah"; zoom the pink dot. Breeches: "Sam Ortiz"; point at the orange dot

Makers and their colors are set up once for your whole organization, on its "Makers" page, so every production shares the same team and the same colors. That color then follows each maker everywhere they are assigned, across every role and every piece.

> navigate directly to the "Makers" page (do not open the Clerk organization popover); hold on the list, colors visible

## The Costume Creations page (`creations-page`)

Everything you are making, across every role, comes together on one "Costume Creations" page for the show. Its "To make" tab lists each piece for each performer, with their measurements close at hand, and the tab's own count shows how many are finished so far.

> click the production's link to Costume Creations; hold on "To make"

Open a piece there to fill in its fabric: the type, the color, the width, and where you plan to buy it.

> expand Doublet for Maya Brooks; type Fabric "Wool suiting", Color "Deep green", Width 60, $/yd 18

## Skirt yardage (`skirt-yardage`)

Skirts get their own calculator. Choose the skirt type, full circle, three-quarter circle, half circle, or gathered, and the app works out the yardage for you from the performer's waist, the skirt length, and the fabric width.

> the Musicians skirt for Rosa Diaz: "Skirt type" "Full circle"; type Length (confirm which hint shows); zoom Yardage filling in

The length starts from the performer's own outseam, so it only needs changing for a shorter or longer skirt. A number you type yourself is never quietly overwritten by the calculator.

## Estimate fabric automatically (`estimate-fabric`)

For everything else, choose "Estimate fabric," and the app suggests a yardage for every piece that does not have one yet, using each garment's name and the performer's measurements. It only fills in the blanks, never touching a number you typed yourself, and every estimate stays fully editable afterward. More descriptive piece names and recorded measurements make the estimate noticeably better.

> click "✨ Estimate fabric"; hold through "Estimating…"; point at the filled Yardage cells (no numbers read aloud)

## What it all costs (`costs`)

The "Shopping" tab adds it all up for the whole show: the fabric still to buy, grouped by type, with where to buy it, and every item you are purchasing instead of making.

> click "Shopping"; point at the fabric table, "Purchased subtotal"

Suppliers with a website on file even become links you can tap straight through to shop. Together it all rolls up into one total that adds the fabric and the purchased pieces together, so you always know what the costumes will cost before opening your wallet.

> zoom "Total (fabric + purchased)"

## Log finished pieces (`made-to-inventory`)

When a piece is finished, whether it was made or bought, tick it as "Made" right there on the "To make" tab.

> "To make": tick Made on Doublet

The app then offers to add it to House Inventory, so a costume you already own is ready to reuse in a future show instead of being built again from scratch. Add it once, and a future production can pull the same piece back out rather than sewing or buying it all over again.

> "Add"; fill "Location" "Rack B"; click "Add to House Inventory"

## Wrap up (`wrap-up`)

That is how you plan the costumes for a show: add each role's pieces with their photos and notes, decide what to make and what to buy, share the work with makers by their color, and let the app work out the fabric and the cost. In the next video, we will look at House Inventory, and see how to reuse what you already own. Thanks so much for watching, and we will see you again in the next video.
